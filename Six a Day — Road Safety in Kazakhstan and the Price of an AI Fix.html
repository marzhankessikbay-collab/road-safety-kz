Kazakhstan · Road traffic deaths
6/ day

About six people die on Kazakhstan's roads every day.

What if AI watched the road instead of the driver?

A social-science look at road traffic deaths in Kazakhstan: why the data hides as much as it shows, what an AI system could actually do, and what it would cost. The journey ends with a verdict.

Who is affected

The victims are mostly young and male. In a forensic study of every road death in the Semey region from 2006 to 2010, 74.5% of victims were men and 64.8% were aged 20–49. Two-thirds died at the scene, before any hospital could help.4 Pedestrians are the other big group: in that study they made up 28.9% of deaths, far more than in comparable European countries.4 These are often a family's main earners, so each death also pushes a household toward poverty.

Where

About 75% of crashes happen inside settlements, but highways are where crashes turn deadly.5 In 2024 the regions with the most deaths were Almaty region (379), Turkestan (269) and Zhambyl (264). All three sit along the busy southern transit corridor.1

At what scale, and how it is changing

Officially recorded crashes rose from 13,300 in 2020 to more than 36,100 in 2025.2 As Section 2 shows, much of that rise comes from changes in how crashes are recorded. The number of deaths is the figure to watch. Between 2018 and 2022 deaths rose 15.9% while recorded crashes fell, so fewer crashes were becoming deadlier ones. Researchers call this a severity paradox.5 Deaths fell to about 2,300 in 2025. That is good news, but one year is not a trend, and the death rate is still about five times Germany's. To meet the UN goal of halving road deaths, Kazakhstan would need to cut them by about 7.4% every year.5

The social-science question: Kazakhstan has spent heavily on AI traffic cameras. Why are road deaths still so high, and who pays the price?

My answer: the state treats road safety mainly as a policing problem (catch violators, collect fines), not as a health and infrastructure problem. That puts the blame on individual drivers. Official statistics blame drivers for 86.8–96.3% of crashes (2020–2022),6 yet about one crash in five is linked to bad roads,2 and the 2026 road budget was cut by 20%.2 The people who pay have the least say over how roads are built: pedestrians, rural drivers far from hospitals, and young working men.

Where the data is silent

  • No legal definition of a road death or a serious injury. The "within 30 days" rule is informal, and there is no basis for separating serious from minor injuries.3 People left disabled by crashes are statistically invisible.
  • Police and hospital records are never joined up.3 Someone who dies in hospital on day 10 may never reach the police count.
  • Crashes without police involvement disappear. Insurers estimated at least 90,000 crashes in 2023 from payout data,6 several times the official count.
  • No GPS coordinates and no data on how much people drive,3 so dangerous spots can only be found roughly, and risk cannot be properly measured.
  • Who falls through the gaps: rural victims who die before reaching hospital, pedestrians in places with no footpaths, and anyone whose crash was settled without police.

Bottom line: I trust the number of deaths to show the general direction. I do not trust year-to-year comparisons of crashes and injuries, or official comparisons with neighbouring countries. Any AI trained on these records will inherit their blind spots, which matters a lot in Section 4.

Current State of Research: five ways of looking at the same road

I group the research by approach, because each one sees the problem differently and so suggests a different fix.

1 · Epidemiology: describing who dies

Public-health researchers study road deaths like a disease: who gets hit, where and how often. Myssayev et al. (2014) used forensic records from Semey and found that most victims were men, pedestrians or people in highway crashes, and that most died at the scene. That points to slow emergency help, not only risky driving.4 Akbar et al. (2026) looked at the whole country. They linked deaths to road design and international road-safety star ratings (iRAP), and described the "severity paradox".5 What we know: who dies and where. The weakness: these studies describe the problem but cannot say which fixes work, and they rely on the same flawed official data.

2 · Political economy: development and motorisation

Kopits and Cropper (2005) studied 88 countries. They found that as a country gets richer, road deaths first rise, because more people buy cars, and fall only after income passes a turning point (about $8,600 per person in 1985 dollars).8 On this view, Kazakhstan is in the dangerous middle phase. The debate: does growth fix the problem by itself, or can good policy bring the turning point sooner? My research question takes the second view.

3 · Enforcement evaluation: do cameras work?

A major Cochrane review of 35 studies found that speed cameras cut injury crashes by 8–50% and deadly or serious crashes by 11–44%. But the evidence was only of moderate quality, and none of the studies came from low-income countries.9 In Kazakhstan, the claims about Sergek come from city governments, not from independent researchers. The gap: I could not find any independent study of AI traffic cameras in Kazakhstan.

The debate: do cameras change how people drive, or only how they drive near the camera? A Belgian study found that drivers slowed by 6.4 km/h at fixed cameras but hardly at all before or after them. They brake for the camera and speed up again, which is called the "kangaroo effect".23 In Bogotá, 90 fixed cameras reduced crashes, but drivers sped up again further down the road, which weakened the result.24 I see the same in Astana: drivers know which parts of the bus lanes Sergek does not cover,25 and they break the rules exactly there. Fixed cameras teach drivers where to behave, not how to drive.

4 · Data science: predicting crashes with machine learning

Reviews of the research find that machine learning can predict fairly well how often crashes happen and how bad they are.10 But Ali, Hussain and Haque (2024) warn that the models are often trained in questionable ways and are weak at predicting the future.11 The weakness: these models need exact crash locations and linked police and hospital records, which Kazakhstan does not have (Section 2).

5 · Political sociology: surveillance and the "technological fix"

Marat and Sutton (2021) compared camera surveillance in Kyiv, Almaty and Bishkek. They show that these systems promise order and a modern image, but avoid deeper political change and face little public oversight.12 The weakness: this research rarely measures whether roads became safer, while road-safety research rarely asks who holds power. The two rarely talk to each other.

The gap my project fills: We know who dies (approach 1) and that cameras can work elsewhere (approach 3). But the AI tools proposed for Kazakhstan need data the country does not have (approach 4), its AI cameras have never been independently tested, and their political risks are discussed apart from safety (approach 5). Nobody has asked what an AI road-safety system would look like if it were built to prevent deaths and judged by lives saved, not by fines collected.

The AI-Assisted Solution: Jol Radar

Start with the people on the road

The AI is already arriving. KazAvtoZhol is testing AI cameras on the Astana–Temirtau (134 km) and Astana–Shchuchinsk highways. They detect pedestrians and animals on the road, wrong-way driving and illegal stops, and they also read licence plates, car makes and colours. For now, they are not used to issue fines automatically.27 In Astana, AI cameras are being tested to catch drivers using phones or not wearing seatbelts.28 In both cases the AI is pointed at drivers. Under a news report about the highway cameras, ordinary drivers commented:

"Better lighting would have been better."
"You'd do better to learn how to build roads properly."
"So they recognise it, and then what? How do they prevent anything? They can only write a fine."
"How will this help the driver if it goes to the services, and the services are 40 km away?"
"The one danger is uneven roads. The car gets thrown all over the road, like on a swing."
"A deer ran out. So what? What use is that to the driver?"

Public comments under a news report about KazAvtoZhol's AI cameras, collected by the author in November 2026 and translated from Russian. They are anonymised and are not a representative sample.

These comments repeat this project's argument in plain words. Detecting a hazard is worthless unless something happens next, and what people want most is roads, light and help that arrives. And cameras that watch drivers are easy to beat: drivers already hold the phone below the camera's view, and they know where the cameras do not reach. So Jol Radar ("road radar") turns the AI around. Instead of watching drivers, it watches the road. It finds potholes, faded markings and dark stretches, and it makes the state's repair work visible. Its rule: every detection must lead to an action a driver can feel, and the AI only does the parts AI is actually good at.

1 · ScanAI: computer visionPhones in vehicles that already drive every day (city buses, KazAvtoZhol patrol cars, Kazpost vans that reach villages) film the road. The AI recognises potholes, cracks, faded markings and broken streetlights, and puts them on a map that updates every week.
2 · ListenAI: language modelThe AI reads complaints in Kazakh and Russian that people choose to send (eOtinish, the 1403 hotline) and puts each one on the same map: "no lighting at km 47", "potholes at the junction".
3 · Rank & fixAI: risk predictionA machine-learning model predicts which defects are most likely to cause a crash, using depth, traffic and crash history. Each gets a public deadline: reported → scheduled → fixed. A lane with an unfixed, recorded defect cannot generate fines for drivers who swerve around it.
4 · AlertAI: hazard detectionKazAvtoZhol's AI cameras already detect animals and stopped cars. Jol Radar takes only the hazard and its location, never plates, and sends it with fresh potholes and ice to 2GIS and Yandex Navigator, so drivers are warned minutes ahead.
5 · Place helpAI: crash-hotspot mappingThe AI maps where deadly crashes cluster and where ambulances arrive slowest, to show where a roadside rescue post would save the most lives. People and budgets then build it.

Prerequisite: Electronic crash forms with GPS, linked to hospital 30-day outcomes, as CAREC recommends.3 Run by: KazAvtoZhol and the Ministry of Transport, together with the Ministry of Health for ambulances. The Interior Ministry is a data partner, not the owner. Reaches: drivers, passengers and pedestrians, starting with the roads and towns of the Almaty region, which has the most deaths (Figure 3) and where KazAvtoZhol already runs an AI traffic pilot on the Almaty–Konaev road.37

Grounded in real cases

Case 1 · Las Vegas: predicting crashes, then warning drivers

Waycare's AI predicted when and where crashes were likely on Interstate 15. Police and message boards then acted at those spots, and crashes fell about 17% in a one-year pilot.13 Lesson for steps 3 and 4: predictions only help when they reach people who act.

Case 2 · India (iRASTE): AI that leads to road engineering

AI warning devices on buses flagged emerging danger spots. Crashes involving 200 equipped Telangana buses fell about 40% in a year,16 and 38 blackspots in Nagpur got engineering redesigns.15 Lesson for steps 3 and 5: the output should be road repair, not fines.

Case 3 · USA and Germany: AI that finds potholes

Detroit, USA: a company called RoadBotics put an ordinary smartphone on a car's windscreen. As the car drove, the phone filmed the road, and AI marked the cracks and potholes. This way, Detroit checked all of its 2,600 miles (about 4,200 km) of roads.31

Germany: a German company, vialytics, does the same thing with phones in city vehicles. Since 2018, more than 500 towns and cities have used it to find potholes.32

Boston, USA (a warning): the city made an app called Street Bump that used drivers' own phones to feel bumps in the road. At first it could not tell a pothole from a manhole cover,33 and it got fewer reports from poorer areas, where fewer people had smartphones and cars.34

Lesson for step 1: put the phones in buses and vans that drive the same routes every day, including villages, instead of relying on volunteers.

In Kazakhstan · Sergek and the new AI cameras

Almaty says Sergek cut road deaths by 40% and brought 44 billion tenge into the city budget. These are government claims, not independent evaluations.17 Drivers learn where the cameras don't reach (Section 3). In Aktobe, Sergek fined drivers for swerving around potholes,26 and the system was also used to track people breaking COVID-19 quarantine.18 The national road-quality centre already uses AI in a mobile lab,35 so the technology is here. What is missing is coverage and follow-through.

The bill: what Jol Radar would cost

JOL RADAR · TOLL RECEIPTRoute: Problem → Verdict · Payer: the public

🌍 EnvironmentalLOW

Jol Radar adds no fixed cameras. Road scanning uses phones already riding in buses and vans, and each road picture is analysed once per pass. That takes far less computing than watching live video 24 hours a day. Sorting complaints and ranking defects run on ordinary servers, not a data centre. On Kazakhstan's grid, where coal-fired plants produced 74.9% of electricity in 2024,20 every extra camera or GPU means more coal burned. KazAvtoZhol's new highway units run on solar panels and wind generators rather than the grid,27 and Jol Radar should require that for any roadside hardware. Rule: no new fixed cameras, and computing hosted in Kazakhstan on existing government servers.

💰 EconomicHIGH

The AI is the cheap part. The scanning equipment is just an ordinary smartphone fixed to the windscreen of a bus or van that already drives the route. The expensive parts are digitising police crash forms, linking them to hospital records, and above all doing the repairs. In January–September 2025, KazAvtoZhol found 2,777 road defects and had fixed only about 1,400.36 Finding more potholes only helps if repair money grows too, and the 2026 road budget was cut to 1.2 trillion tenge against a need of 1.9 trillion.2

Who pays: the state budget. Who profits: tech companies, and city budgets if the system turns into a fine machine. Sergek brought Almaty 44 billion tenge,17 and Astana's new cameras are funded by a private investor who expects a return.30 Who benefits: families who do not lose someone, and drivers who get a repaired road instead of a fine. The biggest new cost is people: rescue crews stationed along highways so help is not 40 km away.

👷 LaborMEDIUM

Police lose paperwork when crash forms go digital. Road engineers and data analysts are needed, and Kazakhstan has few of them. There is also hidden work: someone has to label road images, check map points, follow injured people in hospital for 30 days and correct the AI's mistakes. This "ghost work" is usually low-paid and invisible.21 Bus and post-van drivers become road inspectors without extra work, but they must be told and asked, not secretly turned into sensors. Repair crews get more work, with public deadlines. Jol Radar pays for these jobs openly.

🏛️ Political & governanceHIGH

The biggest risk is function creep: a system built for one purpose is slowly used for another. Sergek, built for traffic, was used to track people during COVID-19.18 Reading licence plates has a real safety use. Since March 2025, toll roads have used average-speed control: cameras record each plate at two points and check how long the car took between them, so drivers cannot just brake at one camera.29 But the same data also shows where every car went and when.27 That is useful for safety, but it could also be used to follow people, so strict rules on who can see it and how long it is kept matter. Control is moving abroad too: Astana's new 22,000-camera system is built by Presight, part of the UAE's G42 group, with 53 billion tenge of its own money.30 So who stores the data, and under whose law, is a real question.

How Jol Radar measures up against the frameworks from Week 12:

  • Kazakhstan's Law on AI (No. 230-VIII, 2025): takes a risk-based approach and bans practices such as unlawful biometric processing and social scoring.22 Jol Radar collects no faces, plates or driver scores.
  • EU AI Act: AI used as a safety component in managing road traffic is listed as high-risk (Art. 6 and Annex III).38 Jol Radar should be treated the same way, with risk checks, human oversight and an audit. It avoids every practice banned under Art. 5.
  • OECD AI Principles 1.1–1.5: well-being, human rights and privacy, transparency, safety and accountability.39 The public repair tracker and the independent university evaluation are how Jol Radar meets transparency and accountability.
  • UNESCO Recommendation (2021): its principles include proportionality and "do no harm", privacy, human oversight, fairness and sustainability.40 Using phones already in buses instead of new cameras is the proportionate choice.
  • Mhlambi (2020), from rationality to relationality: argues that AI should be judged by how it affects relationships and communities, not only individuals.41 That is why Jol Radar starts by listening to drivers and answers to the public, not only to ministries.

Finally, if the same agency both runs the system and evaluates it, nobody checks it independently. That is why evaluation goes to a university.

🔐 Data ethicsHIGH

What data it needs: road images, complaints, crash locations and hospital outcomes, which are health data. Consent: injured people cannot consent at the roadside, so the legal basis must be public health, with names removed and data stored in Kazakhstan. Complaints are used only if people send them to official channels, never taken from social media. Road footage also shows pedestrians and cars, so faces and plates are blurred on the phone before upload.

What could go wrong: police records miss many rural and pedestrian deaths (Section 2). An AI trained on them could send money to well-recorded city junctions and away from the rural highways where people actually die. Complaints have the same bias, because city residents complain online more than village drivers. Both must be adjusted, or the AI will repeat the inequality it was meant to fix.

Verdict: should Kazakhstan do this?

ONLY UNDER CONDITIONS
Not red, not green. Amber: proceed only if the conditions below are met.

The bill shows that the AI is the cheapest and least important part. If it were built only on today's police data, it would point at the wrong roads. If it were run by the people who collect fines, it would drift toward revenue and surveillance. So my answer is not yet, and only if:

  1. Watch the road, not the driver. AI first scans road defects and publishes repair deadlines. A lane with an unfixed, recorded defect cannot generate fines.
  2. Data first. Electronic GPS crash forms and police–hospital linkage come before any model, with a legal definition of road death and serious injury.
  3. Pilot, then evaluate. Start in one region (Almaty region) for two years, with an independent university evaluation measured in deaths and serious injuries.
  4. Money follows the map. A fixed share of the road budget is reserved for the segments the model flags; otherwise the predictions change nothing.
  5. No faces, no plates, no driver scores. These are banned by design, consistent with the AI Law's prohibitions.
  6. No revenue link. Vendors are never paid from fines, and success is never reported in tenge collected.

If these conditions cannot be met, Kazakhstan should skip the AI and spend the money on footpaths, lighting and ambulances, which the evidence already supports. Drivers' own comments say the same: they asked for lighting, smooth roads and help that arrives, not for smarter cameras. Kazakhstan's most urgent road-safety need is accurate records and better roads, not more AI.
